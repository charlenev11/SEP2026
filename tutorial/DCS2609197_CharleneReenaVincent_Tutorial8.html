About Winnie the Pooh

Winnie the Pooh began not in an animation studio but in a nursery. The author A. A. Milne wrote his first Pooh book in 1926, drawing on the stuffed animals owned by his young son, Christopher Robin Milne. The bear's name blended two real-world sources: a black bear called Winnipeg, or "Winnie," a Canadian soldier's mascot who ended up at the London Zoo, and a swan called Pooh from a family holiday. The stuffed bear that inspired the character was a gift to the real Christopher Robin, and the toys that inspired the stories are now on display in the New York Public Library. Milne's Pooh is quietly funny. He is a "bear of very little brain," but the joke is affectionate rather than cruel, because Pooh's simplicity often lets him see through the pretensions of cleverer characters. The stories are gentle, episodic, and full of wordplay. They are set in a forest that feels both wild and safe, and the illustrations by E. H. Shepard gave the characters their original look: a rounder, more naturalistic bear with no red shirt, drawn in delicate pen and ink.



About Donald Duck

Donald Duck arrived eight years after the first Pooh book, in a completely different medium and mood. He debuted on June 9, 1934, in the Disney short The Wise Little Hen. Walt Disney reportedly heard the performer Clarence "Ducky" Nash doing a comic, sputtering duck voice and realized he had found the sound of a character who could be angry, comic, and endearing all at once. Nash voiced Donald for roughly fifty years, and his distinctive, half-intelligible rasp became inseparable from the character. Tony Anselmo, whom Nash himself trained, took over the role in the 1980s. Where Mickey Mouse was designed to be polite, optimistic, and a role model, Donald was the opposite: a character who is impatient, vain, stubborn, and easily humiliated. That imbalance is what makes him so useful to storytellers. Mickey represents how people wish to behave, while Donald represents how they sometimes actually feel.
